The Autopilot Labs Manifesto  ·  Rev. 04  ·  2026

The eight articles.

Eight clauses we've signed our name to — and inserted verbatim into every statement of work. If we ever violate one, the engagement ends that day and every outcome billed during the breach period is refunded, in full, without dispute.

We put it in writing.
Because writing keeps us honest.

Preamble

How a sales pitch
became a contract.

After our second audit in 2024 — a mid-sized Brisbane clinic group that had signed three AI vendors in eighteen months and fired all three — the client's CFO leaned back in her chair and asked a question we hadn't expected. She wanted to know if the promises we'd just made across her conference table could be written into the contract. Not the pricing. The promises. The line about walking away if the audit showed the workflow wasn't ripe. The line about refunding SLA misses without being asked. The line about handing her team's trained weights back on day one of any exit.

We said yes. A week later our lawyer sent back a redline: the four sentences we'd spoken were now a single clause in Schedule 3. That clause became Article I. Seven more followed — one for each scar we'd earned in the preceding two years, each refused engagement, each weekend spent untangling a vendor's pricing model, each conversation with a procurement lead who'd been trained by the market to assume any AI pitch was, somewhere in the fine print, a lie.

This page is that document. Not the legal text — that lives in the contract, in clauses 4.1 through 4.8 — but the why behind each one, told plainly, with the stories we usually only share over coffee. If you're reading this and considering us, it's worth knowing what you'd be signing us to. If you're reading this and you work for us, it's worth remembering what we signed ourselves to.

Signed, on behalf of the firm — Elena Okafor, CEO
I. Article one
On pricing

No seat-based billing. Ever.

Seats punish adoption. The moment you charge per user, your client has a reason to limit who gets to touch the system — and an AI workflow that only the three most senior people can invoke is, in practice, an AI workflow that doesn't run. We've watched this play out in every software category for twenty years, and we refused to let it happen to ours.

So the rule is absolute. You pay for finished work, or you pay nothing. Add a thousand users to the dashboard on a Tuesday. Invite the night shift. Give a read-only seat to the board. It doesn't change the invoice. The only thing we meter is outcomes — a triaged claim, a reconciled entry, a tagged lease — and we only bill the ones that passed SLA.

◆ Example

In Q3 2025 a mid-market lender asked us to cap their "user licences" at 40 seats to control spend. We rewrote the clause on the spot: unlimited seats, A$2.40 per reconciled entry, refunded on miss. Their adoption tripled in six weeks. Their invoice stayed flat. Their CFO now runs Article I internally as a negotiating frame with their other software vendors.

It also changes what our sales team can do. They can't up-sell you seats. They can't "expand the footprint." The only way we grow revenue on an account is by shipping more finished work. Which is exactly the incentive we wanted them to have.

"I've bought enterprise software for eleven years. This is the first contract I've signed where the vendor's revenue actually goes up when my team uses it more. That's not a pricing model — that's an alignment."

II. Article two
On inference cost

No token pass-through.

Inference cost is our problem. You don't see it on the invoice, you don't forecast it, you don't argue with your finance team about why last month's AI bill doubled because a model provider raised prices. If our underlying model gets cheaper, we keep the margin — and pass it on at the next quarterly rate review, in writing, whether you ask us to or not.

This clause exists because we watched a client of ours — before we were their agency — get hit with a 340% month-on-month AI infrastructure bill because a vendor had quietly switched pricing tiers mid-contract and passed the delta through as a "platform adjustment." It took their finance team six weeks of emails to unwind. Six weeks we could have been shipping work.

◆ Example

In December 2025 the inference cost on one of our highest-volume agent loops — legal discovery tagging — dropped 62% because we migrated the workload. We didn't need to tell the client. We did tell the client, issued a 14% rate reduction on their per-outcome price, and filed it under "routine rate review."

The client's general counsel replied: "You just told me you're making less money. Are you sure?" We were sure. Article II.

The practical consequence is that our margins compress when models get expensive and expand when they get cheap — and our clients see only a smooth, predictable per-outcome price. That's the trade. We took on the volatility so your FP&A team doesn't have to model it.

"The first time an AI vendor reduced our rate without us asking, I thought it was a billing error. I called the rep. He sent me clause 4.2. I read it three times."

III. Article three
On refusing work

We turn work down.

If the two-week audit shows the workflow isn't ripe — not enough volume to justify the build, too much human judgement at each step, data in a shape no model can reliably parse — we say so and we walk. Roughly a third of audits end this way. The diagnostic report is free, you keep everything we found, and we pass on the work.

This is the clause our board hates the most and the one our clients value the most. It's also the clause that's held the hardest line: we've walked away from engagements with seven-figure year-one budgets because the underlying workflow couldn't support an agent. Not wouldn't. Couldn't. If we'd taken the work, we'd have been charging a premium to deliver mediocrity, and the reputational cost would have compounded across every future deal.

◆ Example

In February 2025 a listed e-commerce group asked us to automate refund dispute handling across 14 brands. We spent ten days inside their ops team, mapped the workflow, ran a sample of 400 cases — and found that 38% of dispute resolutions required the rep to read the customer's tone across a threaded conversation and make a judgement call about brand goodwill. Tone-reading at that bar, for that stake, isn't agent work. Not yet.

We wrote the report, refunded nothing (the audit was already free), recommended a workflow re-design first, and declined to quote. Six months later they came back with the redesigned process. We shipped the agent in three weeks.

Article III is also how we filter our own pipeline. If an enquiry comes in for a workflow we know from three prior audits isn't ripe, we tell them so on the first call instead of running the audit. We've saved roughly 180 engineering days this way in the last twelve months. Time we spent on work that was ready.

"They walked away from a A$1.4M year one. Eight months later we hired them on a A$600K contract for a narrower scope. I've never trusted a vendor more."

IV. Article four
On human oversight

Humans own the final click.

For every regulated workflow, every high-blast-radius action, every step whose reversal would cost real money or real trust — a named human approves before the agent proceeds. We make that click fast. We don't make it absent. There is no autonomous mode for sending a patient referral, filing with the regulator, or pushing funds.

This is not a compliance gesture. It's a design principle. The failure modes we fear most in AI systems aren't bad outputs — we can test for those — they're confident bad outputs that slip past a half-attention reviewer because the agent always seemed right before. Article IV forces us to make the human's check meaningful. If the reviewer is just rubber-stamping, we've designed the UI wrong, and we rebuild.

◆ Example

In our healthcare intake agent, the final click is "approve and send to referral network." For the first nine months we had a one-click approval. Then we noticed nurses were approving faster than a human could read the summary. We rebuilt the approval UI to require a single diff — "what the agent changed from the original note" — and acknowledgement of any field marked low confidence.

Approval rate dropped from 99.1% to 96.4%. Error rate in referrals dropped by 71%. That 3-point approval dip is the point of the clause.

We publish the "final click" map for every deployed workflow on day one: every step, who approves, what they see, how long the median click takes. Clients can audit it. They often do.

"The agent drafts. My nurses approve. Nobody got replaced — they just stopped doing the tedious half. That's the version of AI I can defend to a board and to a union."

V. Article five
On service levels

SLA misses refund automatically.

No support ticket. No escalation. No forty-minute call with a customer success manager who has to "go check with the billing team." If an outcome misses its SLA — on turnaround, on accuracy, on availability — the credit appears on the next invoice without you asking. The credit line reads exactly what happened. You can reconcile it in thirty seconds.

Auto-refund is the strictest form of this promise we know how to write. It takes away our own ability to negotiate our way out of a miss. It takes away the temptation to negotiate. And it puts a cold financial pressure on our own engineering team: every missed SLA is a line item on the P&L that shows up, every month, in the monday review.

◆ Example

In November 2025 a model provider outage knocked one of our legal discovery agents offline for 4 hours and 11 minutes — crossing the 4-hour SLA threshold. We didn't notify the client's procurement team. We didn't have to. Their December invoice showed a line: "Article V credit — SLA miss, 11 Nov — A$14,820." The client's ops lead emailed us to say it was the first time an enterprise vendor had ever refunded her for something she hadn't noticed was broken.

The credit-back rate sits around 0.9% of billed outcomes across our book. We publish that number every quarter on a simple page our clients can bookmark. If it ever drifts above 2%, we owe the client — and ourselves — an explanation.

"I got a refund in November for an outage I hadn't even opened a ticket for. My procurement team thought I was making it up. I forwarded the invoice."

VI. Article six
On data & exit

Your data leaves when you leave.

Weights fine-tuned on your corpus are yours. Retrieval indices built on your documents are yours. Log streams we ingested, prompt libraries we assembled, evaluation sets we curated — yours. On the day you tell us you're walking away, the clock starts. Inside thirty days, we deliver the artefacts, in documented formats, with an exit runbook written by the engineer who built the system. Inside sixty, we purge our side and give you a signed certificate of destruction.

We wrote this clause after watching a former client of ours — a healthcare network in a different country — try to leave a different AI vendor and discover, six months into the "transition," that the weights had been trained inside a shared multi-tenant model with no extraction path. The network paid a seven-figure fee to unwind it. We wrote Article VI the week we heard the story.

◆ Example

In Q1 2026 a fintech client chose to bring their reconciliation agent in-house after eighteen months with us. We ran the exit on a fixed timeline: day 0 notice, day 14 weights handed over in a signed archive, day 21 a three-day engineering handoff with their new internal team, day 30 deletion certificate, day 45 a standing offer to come back without re-onboarding fees if the in-house path didn't stick.

They haven't come back. We hope they never have to. Article VI isn't a retention tool — it's the opposite. It's a promise we won't hold you hostage if you decide to leave.

The cost of this clause is real. An exit well-supported is an exit that costs us roughly 40 engineering hours. We treat that cost as the price of meaning it. Every client who doesn't leave knows we would have helped them if they had.

"They handed over the weights on day fourteen. The runbook was better than our internal docs. Exiting them was the most professional vendor experience I've had in seven years of CTO work."

VII. Article seven
On scope

We don't sell AGI.

We sell finished tasks in thirteen industries. A tagged lease, a reconciled entry, a triaged claim. That's it. If the job requires general reasoning across unfamiliar domains, open-ended research, or the kind of cross-vertical synthesis that sounds great in a keynote — we're honestly not the shop. The honest answer is worth more than the contract.

The temptation to stretch this clause is constant. A client will describe a workflow that's 80% within our catalogue and 20% over the horizon, and ask if we can just, you know, "have the agent figure the rest out." Every one of our engineers wants to say yes. Article VII is why we say: yes to the 80%, no to the 20%, here's a consultant we trust for the 20%, let's talk in six months about whether the boundary has moved.

◆ Example

A media client asked us last year to build an agent that would "read the news each morning, decide which stories matter to our brand, and draft a CEO memo." We love that client. We said no. The judgement layer — what matters to a specific human brand on a specific day — isn't a workflow we have a reliable loop for. Not in our thirteen verticals. Not yet.

We shipped them a narrower agent: given a curated list of stories a human chose, draft the memo in house voice. It ran for nine months with a 93% publish rate. The CEO still chose the stories.

Article VII is the reason our NPS is high and our addressable market is, on purpose, smaller than it could be. We'd rather be the agency that finishes what it quoted than the agency that quoted everything.

"They turned down the hardest part of the brief and told me who to call for it. Then they shipped the rest in four weeks. Nobody else does that."

VIII. Article eight
On plain language

Plain English, always.

If a clause in our contract needs a lawyer to translate, we rewrite it. The document you sign should be one your ops lead can read on a Sunday night, follow end to end, and audit against the actual service — without legal on the call. We've hit roughly Flesch 58 across the live contract and we're still trimming. Your SLA should not read like an insurance policy.

This is the clause our own lawyer fought hardest. She is an excellent lawyer. She was right about several of the specific edits. She was wrong about the overall direction, and she eventually agreed. Every six months she and our COO sit down with the current contract, print it, read it aloud, and rewrite anything that tripped either of them. The contract is shorter now than it was two years ago, and we ship twelve times the volume of work.

◆ Example

The original Article V — the auto-refund clause — ran to 284 words of nested conditionals describing "eligible qualifying outcomes" and "disputed service event windows." A client's ops manager wrote us an email that said, in full: "What does this mean?"

We rewrote it that week. It's now 71 words. It says: if we said we'd ship it by X time at Y accuracy and we didn't, the credit shows up on your next invoice. Nobody has asked us what it means since.

Plain English isn't a style choice. It's a forcing function. If you can't explain a clause in a sentence, the clause is hiding something — from the client, from yourself, or both. Article VIII is the one that keeps the other seven honest.

"I read every AI contract our firm signs. Theirs is the only one I've sent back with zero redlines. My kids can read it."

Article VIII · plain English
How to invoke an article

If you think we've breached,
here is what you do.

Email manifesto@autopilotlabs.io with a short description of which article you believe we've breached and why. That's the whole first step. We commit — in writing, in clause 4.9 — to a substantive reply from a named partner within 24 hours, any business day. No lawyers required for the first round. No forms. No tickets. No "someone from our customer success team will be in touch."

If we agree you're right, we refund, we document what went wrong, and we publish the root cause to your team. If we think you're wrong, we say why, in plain English, with the logs. If we can't agree after 72 hours, either side can escalate to the dispute process in clause 4.10 — which, for the record, we have invoked zero times in three years.

The article system only works if you use it. Please use it.
