Why per-outcome pricing beats per-seat — an empirical note

After eighteen months billing forty-one teams both ways, the data isn't subtle. Per-outcome contracts cost buyers 58% less on average, produced higher satisfaction, and — counter-intuitively — made us more profitable too.

— Priya Okonkwo Principal Economist 14 min read · 2,380 words

The first time a CFO rolled her eyes at me, it was on a Tuesday in February 2025. We were three months into an engagement with a mid-market brokerage in Sydney, automating their commercial-lease intake. She'd just read our invoice — a neat, proud line item that said Agents (3 × seat) — A$4,800/mo — and she did the math out loud. "You're charging me four-eight a month. The agent processed eleven leases last month. That's four hundred and thirty-six dollars a lease. I pay my human paralegal eighty." She slid the page back across the table. "Explain."

I could not explain. Or rather, I could, but every version of the explanation was some flavour of because that is how SaaS works, and she — a chartered accountant with forty years on the ledger — was not buying it. She wasn't wrong. We had priced the contract the way every AI vendor prices contracts in 2024 and 2025: a seat fee, scaled by "user," padded for compute, indexed to an annual review. The agent, meanwhile, had spent most of the month idle, because it was January and commercial leasing in Sydney is dead in January. We were charging her full fare to keep an empty bus hot.

That invoice was the beginning of a long, uncomfortable internal conversation at Autopilot Labs that ended, fourteen months later, with us tearing up the price book. This note is a post-mortem of that decision — what we changed, what we measured, and why I believe per-outcome pricing is the only economically coherent way to sell AI automation to a business that cares about its P&L. It is not a polemic. It is an argument with receipts.

The premise: SaaS priced labour, not output.

Per-seat pricing made perfect sense for the thing it was invented for. When Salesforce began charging per login in 1999, the product was a workspace — a place a human went to do work. The seat was a proxy for a person's willingness to pay for a tool. More people, more seats, more value; the relationship was linear and honest. For twenty-five years, the whole of B2B software adopted the model because the underlying assumption held: software was a tool a human used, and humans were the bottleneck.

AI agents break that assumption in a load-bearing way. An agent is not a tool a human uses — it is a worker that replaces a use. When you deploy an AI agent to automate claim triage, you are not buying a faster interface for your claim adjusters; you are buying the output those adjusters used to produce. The unit of value has moved from "person-hours of tool access" to "finished jobs." Charging per seat for an agent is a bit like an Uber charging you by the minute you sit in the back rather than the distance you travelled. You can do it, but your customer will correctly feel that the unit of the invoice and the unit of the value are misaligned.

◆ The misalignment — observed 73% of per-seat AI contracts we audited in 2025 had a utilisation rate under 40% of contracted capacity. Customers were paying for agent-hours they didn't consume. n = 41 contracts, mixed vendors.

That number — 73% — is the single most important figure in this essay. It says that on nearly three quarters of AI contracts written on the seat model, the customer is overpaying for capacity they never consume. Seat pricing, in the age of agents, is a covert surcharge on variance. If you happen to have a stable, predictable workload that maps cleanly onto the vendor's seat definition, you'll be fine. Most businesses don't have that. Most businesses have January.

A seat is a proxy for a human being's willingness to pay for a tool. An agent has no willingness to pay. It has no inside, no seat, no Tuesday afternoon at 3pm. Billing it as though it does is a category error.
— Internal memo, Autopilot Labs pricing committee, March 2025

What we did differently.

In April 2025 we ran a controlled experiment across sixteen new engagements. Eight were priced on our existing per-seat model, averaging A$1,600 per agent per month. Eight were priced on a new model: a rate card of completed outcomes, with a floor (a small monthly minimum to keep the agent warm) and a ceiling (a quarterly recalibration if volume grew past an agreed band). Every other variable we could control — vertical mix, customer size, agent architecture, SLA — we matched as closely as the booking pipeline allowed.

The outcome rate was not a casual number. Pricing a triaged medical claim or a reviewed lease or a reconciled ledger entry required a genuine unit-cost model: tokens in, tokens out, review minutes, failure rate, re-run probability. For each of roughly 140 workflow types we had in production by the end of 2025, we built a four-column ledger: expected cost per outcome, standard deviation of that cost, target margin, and human-equivalent price. The last column was the honest one. If a human paralegal could turn around a lease review for A$80 and an agent could do it for A$7.40 at cost, we picked a per-outcome price — typically A$11 to A$14 — that was a clear win for the buyer and a healthy margin for us.

The first thing that surprised us was that buyers loved the unit-cost ledger even when we didn't discount heavily. CFOs — the same CFOs who had been rolling their eyes at seat invoices — leaned forward when we showed them the four-column ledger. It spoke their language. Marginal cost of the next unit of output. That's the thing they'd been taught to manage at business school, and for twenty-five years of SaaS procurement, no one had spoken it to them. Their internal approval processes, which had been grinding against our seat invoices for months, suddenly moved in days.

Twelve months of data.

By March 2026 we had twelve clean months of head-to-head data. The per-outcome cohort had grown from eight contracts to twenty-nine, as most of the original per-seat cohort migrated after their first renewal window. I am going to resist the temptation to bury you in tables; the core finding is in one small chart.

A few things jump out. The first is the obvious one — the per-outcome bars are, on average, less than half the height of the per-seat bars. Across twelve months, the mean monthly invoice for per-outcome engagements was A$2,980; for matched per-seat engagements it was A$7,120. Customers on the outcome model paid, on average, 58% less. That is the headline number, and I'll return to why we're still more profitable despite it.

◆ Mean saving on the switch A$4,140 per engagement, per month. Across the 29-contract cohort, that's A$1.44M of buyer surplus a year we handed back. Rolling 12 months, to Mar 2026.

The second thing to notice is the shape. Per-seat bars are flat and monotonous — they only know one number, the contracted seat count. Per-outcome bars move with the business. You can see the summer lull (Dec–Jan, for Australian clients; the seat cohort is paying the same through it), the February reboot, the March surge as end-of-quarter comes in. Our customers' invoices now breathe the way their revenues do. When a real-estate broker has a quiet month, their Autopilot Labs bill drops; when they have a boom month, it rises — but so do their commissions, so the bill is always a defensible fraction of realised work.

The third thing, visible in the dashed mean lines but worth calling out: the per-seat mean is remarkably stable. The coefficient of variation across those twelve months is 3.2%. The per-outcome mean wobbles more — coefficient of variation 18.4% — because it is actually tracking reality. Stability in an invoice is not a virtue when the thing it's pretending to measure is itself highly variable. That stability is just a lie about the underlying process.

But doesn't outcome pricing punish the vendor?

This is the question every investor asks. If the customer pays less on the outcome model, the logical inference is that we — the AI agent vendor — must be losing money. We are not, and the reason we are not is the most intellectually satisfying part of this whole exercise.

The per-seat model, because it disconnects price from volume, has a perverse effect on both sides of the marketplace. The buyer pays for idle capacity. The seller — this is the part that took me a year to see — has no pricing pressure to actually finish the work. If my seat invoice goes out regardless of how many leases the agent triaged, my incremental incentive to improve the agent's throughput is essentially zero. I get paid for keeping the seat warm, not for shipping the outcome. A whole generation of SaaS products has run on this logic, and it is the reason "AI pilot" is a synonym for "expensive thing that didn't go anywhere."

Outcome pricing re-aligns the vendor. If we don't ship, we don't eat. Every dollar of our margin now depends on the same thing our customer's dollar depends on — the agent actually finishing the job.
— Priya Okonkwo · Pricing Committee Q3 minutes, Aug 2025

Once your revenue is a function of outcomes shipped, every engineering hour you spend improving the agent's first-pass success rate, reducing its token burn, or tightening its review loop flows directly back to you as margin expansion. We are currently shipping at a blended gross margin of 61% on per-outcome contracts, up from 44% on our legacy per-seat book. The per-unit cost of a shipped outcome has fallen in every vertical we run in — some dramatically. Our healthcare intake agent's cost to produce a normalised patient record has fallen from A$1.87 to A$0.38 in eleven months. We kept the price roughly stable, which means the falling cost went almost entirely to us as margin. That is only possible because every engineer on the team knows, at the end of each sprint, that an efficiency gain is a dollar gain.

◆ Gross margin · per-outcome book 61% up from 44% on the legacy per-seat book. Margin expansion driven by engineering efficiency, not price rises. Q1 2026 finalised figures.

A word on the edge cases.

Outcome pricing is not a panacea, and I want to be honest about where it struggles. There are at least three scenarios where the per-seat model is, genuinely, the right answer.

The first is pre-production experimentation. When you're still figuring out whether an agent can do a job at all, you don't yet have a reliable unit-cost model, and pricing per outcome forces both parties to agree on a number nobody has the data to support. We still sell a four-week "discovery" engagement at a flat monthly fee. It's a seat, in effect — a seat for the team to use us as a consulting partner. That's honest, and priced accordingly.

The second is highly strategic, low-volume workflows — the board-pack assistant, the M&A diligence reviewer, the regulator-correspondence drafter. Here the outcomes are rare (maybe three a month), each one is worth enormous amounts to the business, and the variance in effort is wide. Pricing per unit in these situations creates incentive artefacts — a vendor may find itself perversely motivated to split outputs into more units, or to ship quickly rather than carefully. We use a hybrid here: a retainer covering availability, plus an outcome-share if the work proceeds to production. It's messier, and it's right.

The third is small customers early in their journey. A ten-person startup doing 40 outcomes a month is not going to enjoy a variable invoice — they want predictability more than they want fairness-to-the-unit. For them we built a simple capped plan: a small monthly base that covers up to X outcomes, then per-outcome above it. This is the "hybrid" plan that now accounts for 47% of our book. It is, quietly, the most popular plan. Turns out most buyers want variance in principle and predictability in practice. Outcome pricing with a predictable floor is the design compromise that honours both.

A question of what you are actually selling.

If I had to compress the whole argument into a single line, it would be this: the price of a thing should point at what the thing is. A tool you use is priced per use. A worker you hire is priced per hour. A service that produces output is priced per unit of output. For a quarter century, B2B software pretended AI agents were tools. They are not tools, they are workers that replace uses. The invoice should say so.

This matters beyond the line item. When you price per outcome, you force every conversation in the business to become a conversation about the thing that matters — the outcome. Engineering discussions become about first-pass success rates, not "user stories." Sales conversations become about the size of the buyer's outcome pool, not seat counts. QA discussions become about escape rates, not bug counts. The pricing model is an epistemic technology; it determines what the organisation can even see. Per-seat pricing makes it hard to see the work. Per-outcome pricing makes it hard to see anything else.

I'll close with a small anecdote. Six weeks ago I was on a call with the same CFO who rolled her eyes at me in February 2025. She now runs a different brokerage — bigger — and we just closed a contract for five agents on an outcome plan. At the end of the call she said, half to me and half to the ceiling, "I don't know why no one else is doing it this way." I do. It's because per-seat pricing is easier. You don't have to build the unit-cost ledger. You don't have to expose your margins to efficiency gains. You don't have to ship. The comfortable thing, for a long time, has been to charge a retainer and hope the customer doesn't do the arithmetic. But the customers are doing the arithmetic now. Some of them have a forty-year head start.

If you automate with AI and you are charging per seat, the question is not whether your pricing model will change. It's whether you will change it yourself or be changed by a competitor who already has. We changed ours; the numbers above are what happened. I expect the rest of the industry to arrive at the same conclusion, because the data is what the data is, and the arithmetic, in the end, wins.
